Why it matters

Nine reasons to know your watt-hours per million tokens

The number is small, cheap to buy and easy to ignore. It is also the only figure that ties a model, a GPU and a workload to a power feed, a carbon line and a utilisation rate at the same time. Here is why each of those matters, with the working shown.

1

It is a utilisation meter

Take one model on one pair of GPUs and change nothing but how many requests the server holds at once. On the index, Qwen 3 32B on two H100 cards goes from {{L32_FIRST}} Wh/M at a running batch of {{L32_FIRST_BATCH}} to {{L32_BEST}} Wh/M at {{L32_BEST_BATCH}}. That is {{L32_RATIO}}× on the same hardware. The cards' draw rises {{L32_POWER_PCT}} percent, from {{L32_FIRST_W}} W to {{L32_BEST_W}} W, while the tokens they produce each second rise {{L32_TPS_RATIO}}×, and median inter-token latency stays at {{L32_MAX_ITL}} ms or under.

Read it the other way round. A high energy per token does not mean the GPU is working hard. It means the GPU is drawing close to full power and producing few tokens, which is what an under-used server looks like from the wall socket. Energy per token is the utilisation rate expressed in a unit finance can see. If yours is high, you are paying for silicon that is waiting.

Source: ML.ENERGY Leaderboard v3.0, Text Conversation, all {{L32_COUNT_WORD}} measured configurations of Qwen 3 32B on 2 × H100. Full ladder on the index.

2

Power is the ceiling

A data hall, a sovereign site or a telco exchange has a fixed power envelope long before it has a fixed GPU count. Energy per token converts that envelope into capacity. One megawatt-hour serves {{TOKENS_PER_MWH_REF_B}} billion output tokens at the reference row ({{REF_WH_1}} Wh/M). At the top of the concurrency ladder, {{LADDER_32_FIRST}} Wh/M, the same megawatt-hour serves {{TOKENS_PER_MWH_TOP_B}} billion.

An eight-GPU DGX B200 is rated at about {{DGX_B200_KW}} kW, and each B200 can be configured up to {{B200_TDP}} W. Those are the numbers a facility engineer works with. What they buy depends entirely on the Wh/M the platform achieves, which is why the figure belongs in the capacity plan and not only in the sustainability report.

Sources: ML.ENERGY Leaderboard v3.0 for the rows; NVIDIA DGX B200 product page and the HGX B200 product carbon footprint summary (July 2025) for the ceilings.

3

The board will ask

Under the European Sustainability Reporting Standards, disclosure E1-5 asks for total energy consumption in MWh from own operations, split by fossil, nuclear and renewable sources. AI served on GPUs the company runs is a new and growing line inside that total, and its electricity lands in Scope 2. Tokens bought from a hosted API are different: that electricity is the provider's Scope 2 and the buyer's Scope 3 category 1 (purchased goods and services), and it sits outside the buyer's E1-5 MWh. A company that cannot say how many MWh its assistants used last quarter, or how many they will use next year as adoption grows, cannot fill in the line with a method it can defend.

Energy per million tokens is the forecasting unit in both cases. Tokens served next year multiplied by Wh/M gives the MWh. Without the first factor the second is a guess.

Sources: Commission Delegated Regulation (EU) 2023/2772, ESRS E1-5 "Energy consumption and mix" (energy consumption related to own operations) and E1-6 (Scope 2, location-based and market-based); GHG Protocol Scope 3, category 1, purchased goods and services.

4

The regulator already asks model providers

The EU AI Act (Regulation 2024/1689) requires providers of general-purpose AI models to keep technical documentation, and Annex XI lists what it must contain. Section 1, point 2(e) names the "known or estimated energy consumption of the model", and adds that where it is unknown it may be based on the computational resources used. The duty sits with the model provider, not with the company deploying the model.

That is the point. Once a property is documented upstream, buyers start asking for it downstream. A deployer choosing between two models, or between a hosted API and its own cluster, will want the energy figure at their own load and not the provider's. The index is a public baseline to check that answer against.

Source: Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, Article 53(1)(a) and Annex XI, Section 1, point 2(e).

5

Location changes carbon {{SAU_NOR_RATIO}}×, and not energy

Energy per token is a property of the model, the hardware and the load. Carbon per token adds the grid. On Ember's {{EMBER_YEAR}} annual averages, Saudi Arabia's grid runs at {{SAU_G}} gCO2e per kWh and Norway's at {{NOR_G}}. The reference row priced against them gives {{REF_KG_SAU}} kg CO2e per billion tokens in one place and {{REF_KG_NOR}} kg in the other, for identical tokens on identical GPUs.

So a sovereign or in-country deployment changes the carbon figure with no change to the energy figure, in either direction. Which is why the two numbers should be reported separately, and why a claim about one should never be dressed as a claim about the other.

Source: Ember, Yearly electricity data, September 2026 release, emissions intensity of total generation, {{EMBER_YEAR}}: a lifecycle, generation-based figure for comparing places, not a Scope 2 combustion factor to file. {{GRID_COUNT}} countries in the API; twenty-two on the index.

6

It is the E in the SCI

ISO/IEC 21031:2024 standardises the Software Carbon Intensity specification: SCI = ((E × I) + M) per R, where E is the energy consumed by the software, I the carbon intensity of that energy, M the embodied emissions of the hardware, and R the functional unit. For a language model, R is naturally a million output tokens. E is the energy of all the hardware reserved for the service; this index supplies the GPU part of it, which is a floor. I is the location-based grid intensity, which Ember supplies. M is an amortised share of the embodied emissions, not a total: TE × (TiR / EL) × (RR / ToR).

NVIDIA's {{B200_EMBODIED_KG}} kg CO2e cradle-to-gate figure for an eight-GPU HGX B200 baseboard covers the baseboard only; it excludes the host, the use phase and end of life. As an illustration, spread over {{SCI_LIFE_YEARS}} years of continuous use it comes to {{SCI_G_PER_H}} g an hour. {{SCI_MODEL}} on {{SCI_GPUS}} serves {{SCI_MTOK_PER_H}} million output tokens an hour at its index row, so M is about {{SCI_M_PER_M}} g per million tokens, against E × I of {{SCI_EI_NOR}} g on the Norwegian grid. The {{SCI_LIFE_YEARS}}-year life is an assumption for the illustration, not a source figure.

Anyone building an SCI score for an AI service needs the E. The index is a public place to get a baseline for its GPU part.

Sources: ISO/IEC 21031:2024, Information technology, Software Carbon Intensity (SCI) specification; NVIDIA, Product Carbon Footprint Summary for HGX B200, July 2025.

7

Electricity per token is cheap. That is exactly why the number matters.

At the reference row, a million output tokens takes {{REF_WH_1}} Wh, which at USD {{ELECTRICITY_PRICE}} per kWh is {{CENTS_PER_M_REF}} US cents. A billion tokens is about USD {{USD_PER_B_REF}}. Nobody will make a decision on that bill.

But the bill is not the point. Energy per token is the power a GPU draws multiplied by the time it spends on the tokens. Watt-hours per token track GPU-hours per token, and GPU-hours are the real cost of serving, whether they are rented at cloud rates or depreciated on a balance sheet. A configuration that halves Wh/M has halved the GPU-hours per token. The electricity figure is the cheapest instrument you can put on the most expensive thing in the room.

Working: {{REF_WH_1}} Wh per million tokens is {{REF_KWH_PER_B}} kWh per billion; at USD {{ELECTRICITY_PRICE}} per kWh that is USD {{USD_PER_B_REF}} per billion tokens, or {{CENTS_PER_M_REF}} cents per million. The electricity price is an assumption for the worked example, not a source figure.

8

Sliced GPUs are unmeasured

Multi-Instance GPU lets one A100, H100, H200 or B200 be split into up to seven isolated slices, and it is how many enterprise and telco platforms serve several small models from one card. NVIDIA publishes the slice shapes. It does not publish power draw per slice, and neither of the two public benchmarks on the index has measured a sliced card. So the configuration a great many production deployments actually run in has no public energy figure at all.

That is the first gap Lead Green intends to close with a measurement of its own: a 32B model on two MIG slices against a whole card, GPU power sampled through NVIDIA DCGM, with hardware, driver, engine and prompt set named. Until it is published, the honest answer for a sliced deployment is "unknown, and above the index".

Source: NVIDIA Multi-Instance GPU User Guide, supported MIG profiles, last updated 11 September 2026. Profiles on the index.

9

What to do on Monday

  1. Ask the vendor for Wh per million output tokens at your concurrency. Not at theirs. Name the model, the precision, the GPU count, the workload and the latency limit, and ask for the average running batch the figure was measured at (the number of requests the server was decoding at once, not the queue). Workload matters as much as load: on the same leaderboard, the Problem Solving task costs {{GPQA_RATIO_MIN}} to {{GPQA_RATIO_MAX}} times the energy per response of Text Conversation for the same model on the same GPU, because its answers run {{GPQA_OUT_MIN}} to {{GPQA_OUT_MAX}} tokens against {{CHAT_OUT_MIN}} to {{CHAT_OUT_MAX}}. If the vendor cannot answer, the index gives you the floor to hold them to.
  2. Measure your own. NVIDIA DCGM or nvidia-smi report GPU power; sample it at one hertz through a steady load, divide the energy by the output tokens served, and you have your own row. The measurement protocol is short and the result can be listed beside the public ones.
  3. Put the number in the RFP. One clause, below, turns a marketing conversation into a comparable figure across every bidder.

Source for the workload comparison: ML.ENERGY Leaderboard v3.0, Problem Solving (gpqa) against Text Conversation, energy-optimal rows at or under a {{GPQA_ITL}} ms median inter-token latency, the leaderboard's own default for both tasks; no measured configuration exceeds {{MAX_CHAT_ITL}} ms, so the limit changes no row; {{GPQA_PAIRS}} model and GPU pairs measured on both.

RFP clause, ready to paste

"The bidder shall state the GPU energy per million output tokens (Wh/M) for each proposed model and serving configuration, measured at steady state with the proposed serving engine on the proposed hardware, at an average of [N] concurrent requests and with median inter-token latency not exceeding [T] ms, on a conversational workload with prompts and outputs representative of the buyer's use. The bidder shall name the measurement method, the sampling tool, the software versions and the date, and shall state whether host, network and cooling energy are included. Figures for sliced (MIG) configurations shall be measured on the sliced configuration and not derived from whole-card figures."

Fill [N] with the concurrency you expect in production and [T] with the latency your users will accept. The index uses {{ITL}} ms, the leaderboard's default, which no measured configuration reaches; choose [T] from what your users will tolerate. Everything else in the clause is there so that two bidders' numbers can be compared.
