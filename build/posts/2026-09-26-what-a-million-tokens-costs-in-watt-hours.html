Look, everybody in this industry now buys tokens. Per million, on an invoice, from a cloud or from their own GPUs. And nobody gets told what a million of them costs in electricity. I have sat in enough capacity meetings to know the question is coming, so this is the baseline: what the public measurements say a million output tokens takes in watt-hours, and what moves the number.

The unit

Watt-hours per million output tokens, Wh/M. It is GPU energy only, divided by the tokens the model produced. Prompt processing is inside the figure, because the measurement divides total GPU energy by output tokens. Host, network, cooling and idle time are not inside it. So every number here is a floor.

For scale: a million output tokens is roughly three quarters of a million words of English. And the reference row below, {{REF_WH_1}} Wh, is a {{KETTLE_KW}} kW kettle running for {{KETTLE_MINUTES_REF}} minutes. The kettle is my assumption, not a source; the tokens are.

Where the numbers come from

The ML.ENERGY Leaderboard, run by the ML.ENERGY Initiative at the University of Michigan, serves open-weight models with vLLM on real H100 and B200 servers, measures GPU energy at steady state with its Zeus library, and publishes every configuration it ran as raw data under Apache 2.0. Version 3.0 was updated on 23 September 2026. I took its Text Conversation task, kept the {{CONFIG_COUNT}} configurations it published, and for each model and GPU picked the one with the least energy per token at or under a median inter-token latency of {{ITL}} ms, the leaderboard's own default. None of them comes near it. That gives {{ROW_COUNT}} rows for {{MODEL_COUNT}} models. My recomputation matches the leaderboard's own displayed table, row for row.

See, this is the part the invoice hides. A GPU is a bus. The diesel per trip is about the same whether it carries eight people or two hundred and fifty; the diesel per passenger is not. An enterprise assistant that serves a handful of people at a time is the bus with eight people on it, and it is by far the most common deployment I see.

What that does to the bill, and to the carbon

Electricity first, because people always ask and the answer is small. {{REF_WH_1}} Wh per million tokens is {{REF_KWH_PER_B}} kWh per billion. At USD {{ELECTRICITY_PRICE}} per kWh, my assumption and not a source figure, a billion tokens is USD {{USD_PER_B_REF}} of electricity, or {{CENTS_PER_M_REF}} cents per million. Nobody will change a decision over that. The number matters because it tracks GPU-hours, and GPU-hours are where the money actually goes. Halve the watt-hours per token and you have halved the GPU time per token, on the same kit.

Carbon depends on where the socket is. Multiply Wh/M by the grid's grams of CO2e per kWh and divide by a thousand, and you get kilograms per billion tokens. On Ember's {{EMBER_YEAR}} annual averages the reference row is {{REF_KG_SAU}} kg per billion tokens in Saudi Arabia ({{SAU_G}} g/kWh), {{REF_KG_SRB}} kg in Serbia ({{SRB_G}}), and {{REF_KG_NOR}} kg in Norway ({{NOR_G}}). Same tokens, same cards, {{SAU_NOR_RATIO}}× apart. Location moves the carbon and does nothing to the energy, which is why the two should never be reported as one number.

What I am not telling you

Closed-weight models served over an API. The leaderboard cannot measure them, and neither can anyone outside those companies, so I say nothing about them. Sliced GPUs: NVIDIA does not publish power per MIG slice and no public benchmark has measured one, which is the first gap we will fill with a measurement of our own, on our own rig, with the protocol and the logs published. And the lightly loaded reality: every row on the index is the best case under a latency limit. Your deployment sits above it. The concurrency table tells you how far.

I wish I could tell you the platform we build already measures this for every tenant. It does not, yet. When it does, you will read the number here first, labelled as ours, beside the public ones.

What to do with it

Ask your vendor for Wh per million output tokens at your concurrency, not theirs. Measure your own with DCGM; it is an afternoon's work. And put the number in the RFP. The clause is on the why page. If two bidders give you the same figure at the same load, you have learned something. If neither can, you have learned more.
